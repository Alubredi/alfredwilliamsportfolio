# The Dramaturgy of Distance: Christopher Anyokwu’s “The Far Country” and Modern African Theatricality

**Category:** Cultural & Literary  
**Date:** September 28, 2026  
**Read Time:** 10 min read  
**Author:** Alfred E. Williams  

> **Executive Excerpt:**

## Executive Key Takeaways

- Anyokwu’s play subverts the conventional "Japa" migration trope by confronting the spiritual alienation and psychological amputations of exile.
- The staging at the Unilag Arts Theatre utilized indigenous Yoruba ritual pacing to disrupt Western linear dramaturgy.
- True cultural criticism must engage performance as living civic discourse rather than an ornamental academic exercise.

---

## Investigation Content

The University of Lagos Arts Theatre has long served as a consecrated proving ground for African dramaturgy. Within its storied amphitheatre, where Wole Soyinka, Femi Osofisan, and Bode Sowande have staged dialogues on statecraft and tyranny, Prof. Christopher Anyokwu’s *The Far Country* made its premier stage appearance. To witness its opening production was to experience a profound theatrical meditation on displacement, memory, and the tragic illusions of geographical escape.

      
## Subverting the Romance of Migration

      
In contemporary Nigerian socio-political parlance, migration is celebrated under the collective moniker of *"Japa"*—a rapid exodus framed as the ultimate triumph of personal aspiration over systemic dysfunction. Anyokwu’s dramatic vision rejects this simplistic binary. In *The Far Country*, the promised land of the diaspora is neither an Eden nor a hell; it is an emotional purgatory where identity is continuously interrogated and eroded.

      
> "The protagonist discovers that crossing an ocean does not extinguish the fires of home; it merely deprives the exile of the rain required to put them out."

      
## Ritual Pacing vs. European Realism

      
What distinguished the stage execution was its deliberate departure from standard naturalistic European staging. The dialogue resonated with heavy lyrical weight, punctuated by ancestral chorus interruptions and percussive transitions. The lighting design, bathed in stark amber and midnight blue, cast long architectural shadows across the actors, mirroring the existential liminality of their characters.

      
## The Responsibility of the Literary Critic

      
Criticism in the African literary landscape is frequently enfeebled by either sycophantic praise or sterile academic jargon. As I documented in my original review for *My World of Literature (TWOL)*, a theatrical critic’s true allegiance must be to the integrity of the work’s thematic courage. Anyokwu’s drama forces audiences to confront uncomfortable truths about nationhood, artistic duty, and why the farthest country an African can wander into is often the estranged landscape of his own forgotten heritage.
